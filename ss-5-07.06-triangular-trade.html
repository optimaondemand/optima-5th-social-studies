📚 A Trade Route Built on Human Suffering

The triangular trade was a web of routes connecting Europe, Africa and the Americas across the Atlantic Ocean. On the first leg, ships from ports like Bristol and Liverpool in Britain carried goods made in workshops and mills (cloth, guns, metal tools and alcohol) to the coast of West Africa. There, European traders bought captives from African traders and rulers, who often took them in wars and raids. On the second leg, called the Middle Passage, enslaved Africans were forced onto ships and carried across the Atlantic to the Americas. People were chained below deck and crowded together, with barely enough food and water, for weeks or months. About 1 in every 7 died on the way, from disease, cruelty and hopelessness.

On the third leg, crops grown by enslaved people (sugar, molasses, tobacco, rice and indigo) were shipped to Europe and sold for a big profit, the money left over after the costs are paid. (Cotton came much later, after 1793.) The trade made merchants (traders), colonial planters and whole nations rich. But the profit was built on the forced work and suffering of millions of Africans. Historians estimate that about 12.5 million Africans were forced onto slave ships. Many more died before they ever reached the ships; no one knows how many. No honest editor can call this “ordinary business.”

The triangle is a simple picture of something messier. Many ships sailed only part of it. New England ships carried rum to Africa and brought molasses home from the Caribbean, and Rhode Island merchants sent more slave-trading voyages than any other colony. Ships from Brazil sailed straight to Africa and back.

What the Herald Reports

⚠️ The Harm to People
What the Herald Leaves Out
The second leg, the Middle Passage, forced enslaved Africans across the ocean in terrible conditions. This was not ordinary business.
Evidence About 12.5 million Africans forced onto ships · Chained below deck for weeks or months · About 1 in 7 died on the way · Families torn apart · Many more died before reaching the ships

The Hasty Herald made the triangular trade sound like a business deal. It was a trade network, yes, but one with the Middle Passage at its center. A responsible editor reports both the money and the harm done to people, because the people who suffered deserve an honest account.

A real voice from the past: Olaudah Equiano

Equiano wrote that he was taken from West Africa as a boy. He survived the Middle Passage, later bought his freedom, and in 1789 published his life story in London to help end the slave trade. Here is what he wrote about the ship:

“…a multitude of black people of every description chained together, every one of their countenances expressing dejection and sorrow…”

“The closeness of the place, and the heat of the climate, added to the number in the ship, which was so crowded that each had scarcely room to turn himself, almost suffocated us.”

Olaudah Equiano, The Interesting Narrative of the Life of Olaudah Equiano, Vol. I, chapter 2 (London, 1789).

Words to know: countenances means faces; dejection means deep sadness; closeness means crowding with no fresh air; scarcely means barely; suffocated means choked, unable to breathe.

Some historians question whether Equiano was born in Africa or in South Carolina, because of a few later records. Either way, his book is one of the most important records of the Middle Passage.

🔤 The Word Desk: merc means “trade”

Many English words come from the Latin word merx, which means goods for sale. You can spot it as merc or merce.

  • merchant (merc + hant): a trader who buys and sells goods to make money
  • commerce (com + merce): buying and selling between people and places (com means together)
  • merchandise (merc + handise): goods that are bought and sold
  • commercial (com + merc + ial): having to do with buying and selling

Discovery: In the triangular trade, ship clerks wrote down captured people as if they were merchandise. That is why an honest editor checks the words.

Try it: use the word merchant in a sentence about the triangular trade.

📊 Texas Connection — Free Enterprise in the Colonial Economy (TEKS 10)

Texas standards highlight how the free-enterprise system (people owning businesses and deciding what to make and sell) developed in the colonies: how colonists made goods, traded across the Atlantic, and built an economy that later outgrew British control. The triangular trade shows both the power and the deep unfairness of early trade: enormous wealth was made, but much of it depended on the forced work of enslaved Africans. Understanding how economies work helps us understand why people fought to change them.
