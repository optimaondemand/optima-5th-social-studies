🗞️ Political Participation: Then and Now

Let’s compare who could take part in government in the 1770s and 1780s with who can today.

🗞️
Then vs. Now

As you read each section, notice who was included and who was excluded — and how that has changed over time.

🏛️ Then (1770s–1790s)
  • Who could vote: Mostly white men who owned property or paid taxes
  • Who could not vote: Women and free Black men in most states (a few states let some vote), enslaved people, Native Americans, men without property
  • How people participated: Town meetings, petitions, pamphlets, protests, boycotts, militia service
  • Key limitation: Power was held by a small group. Most people had no vote.
🗳️ Now (Today)
  • Who can vote: Almost all U.S. citizens 18 and older, whatever their race, gender or property
  • How rights expanded: 15th Amendment (race, 1870), 19th Amendment (women, 1920), Voting Rights Act (1965), 26th Amendment (age 18, 1971). An amendment is a change added to the Constitution.
  • How people participate: Voting, contacting representatives, peaceful protest, community organizing, running for office, jury duty
  • Ongoing questions: Voter access, representation, and whether all voices are equally heard
⚖️
Cause — Choice — Consequence

Cause: In the founding era, voting was limited to a small group, mostly white men who owned property.

Choice: Over centuries, Americans fought to expand who counts as "We the People" — through amendments, movements, and laws.

Consequence: Political participation today looks very different from 1776 — but the work of expanding it is ongoing.

🗺️ Participation at a Glance

Political participation has expanded dramatically — but it took centuries of effort.

🦉🗞️
In the 1780s, only a small share of people could vote: about 6 in 100, by one estimate. Today, almost every citizen 18 or older can, but it took nearly 200 years and several amendments to get there.
💡
Key Concept

Political participation has changed dramatically since the founding era. The Herald's claim that it "means the same thing in every time period" ignores centuries of struggle to expand who gets a voice in American democracy.

🔤 The Word Desk: demo means “people”

Many English words come from the Greek word demos, “the people.” You can spot it as demo.

  • democracy (demo + cracy): government by the people
  • democratic (demo + cratic): run by the people
  • demographic (demo + graphic): about groups of people

Discovery: In 1789, only some of the people could vote. Each change let more of the people rule.

Try it: use the word democracy in a sentence about today’s lesson.
