📚 Fertile Land, Diverse People, and Tolerance

The Middle Colonies were New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania and Delaware. They sat between New England and the Southern Colonies, along the Hudson and Delaware rivers. The soil in the river valleys was deep and rich, and summers were longer and warmer than in New England. Farmers grew so much wheat, corn, rye and oats that the region was called “the breadbasket colonies.” Mills ground the grain into flour, and ships carried it from New York City and Philadelphia to the Caribbean islands and to Europe.

The Middle Colonies were also the most diverse region, which means they had people of many different kinds. The Dutch started New Netherland in 1624; the English took it over in 1664 and renamed it New York. Swedes settled along the Delaware River in 1638. In 1681 the King of England gave the land of Pennsylvania to William Penn, a Quaker (a member of a Christian group that believed in peace and plain worship). Penn’s 1701 Charter of Privileges let people who believed in God worship freely, but only Christians could hold a government job. English, German, Welsh, Scots-Irish and Jewish settlers came, and many Germans and Scots-Irish worked for several years to pay off the cost of the ocean trip. Rhode Island and Maryland also had laws about freedom of worship, but Pennsylvania drew the widest mix of people. By the 1760s, Philadelphia was the biggest city in the thirteen colonies, with printers, newspapers, a library and a school.

Not everyone shared in this. Slavery was legal in all the Middle Colonies, and enslaved Africans worked on farms, in homes and on the docks. The land also belonged to the Lenape people first. Penn bought land from Lenape leaders with written agreements called deeds, starting in 1682. But in 1737, after Penn died, his sons used an old, unsigned deed and hired fast walkers to claim land “as far as a man could walk in a day and a half.” This was the Walking Purchase. The Lenape lost about 1,200 square miles of land and were pushed off it.

🤝 Society
Diversity and Tolerance
The Middle Colonies had people from more homelands and more religions than any other region. Pennsylvania’s freedom of worship was wider than in most other colonies (Rhode Island was another).
Evidence Dutch, Swedish, Quaker, German, Scots-Irish and Jewish settlers · Penn’s 1701 Charter of Privileges (for people who believed in God) · Philadelphia, full of people from many countries · Free and enslaved Africans

The Hasty Herald said the Middle Colonies mattered only because of their location. Their spot on the coast did help trade. But rich soil, grain and flour, people from many homelands, and a growing city like Philadelphia made the region matter for its own reasons, and not everyone shared in it. REAL SOURCE FROM THE PAST: a visitor’s words

“…that colony possesses great liberties above all other English colonies, inasmuch as all religious sects are tolerated there. We find there Lutherans, Reformed, Catholics, Quakers, Mennonists or Anabaptists, … Presbyterians, … Jews …”

Gottlieb Mittelberger, Journey to Pennsylvania (he lived there 1750–1754; English translation by Carl Theo. Eben, 1898, pages 54–55). A sect is a religious group. The … marks show words left out. He says “all” sects, but Pennsylvania’s law protected only people who believed in God.
EXAMPLE WRITTEN FOR THIS LESSON (not a real quote from the past)

The soil along the Hudson and Delaware rivers is deep and rich. Wheat, corn, rye and oats grow in such plenty that farmers send shiploads of flour to other colonies and across the sea. The region has earned the name “the breadbasket.”

The people here come from many nations: Dutch, English, Swedish, German, Scots-Irish, Jewish and African, both free and enslaved. In Pennsylvania, the law lets people who believe in God worship freely, so Quakers, Lutherans and Mennonites worship near one another.

Philadelphia is one of the largest cities in British North America, a place of printing presses, a lending library, and people who join in running their city.

Written for this lesson to sum up the facts above. It is not words from the 1700s.

🔤 The Word Desk: col and cult mean “farm”

Many English words come from the Latin word colere, which means to farm, grow or settle. You can spot it as col or cult.

  • colony (col + ony): a settlement, from the Latin word for a farmer
  • cultivate (cult + iv + ate): to prepare land and grow crops
  • agriculture (agri + cult + ure): farming (agri means field)
  • culture (cult + ure): the way a group of people lives, first meaning “the growing of something”

Discovery: The word colony comes from the Latin colonus, a farmer. Many colonists were farmers, and the Middle Colonies farmed so well they were called the breadbasket colonies.
