At a Glance

Unit 8 follows the road from the French and Indian War to the Declaration of Independence. It starts with a war the colonists fought on Britain’s side and asks what changed afterward. Your student learns to look for the chain of events between big moments, instead of jumping straight from one date to the next.

History skill
Find the chain, not just the dates. What happened, what happened because of it, and how do we know? Your student also checks what a source can and can’t show (the lessons call this a limitation), and notices when a picture was made compared with the day it shows.
Virtues
  • Courage (5.08.01): printing what the evidence shows, even when the true story is harder to tell than the simple one.
  • Courage (5.08.02): taking the colonists’ complaints seriously without making them simpler than they were, and staying calm when evidence is missing.
  • Courage (5.08.03): reporting every side of the story, even when it would be easier to pretend everyone agreed.
  • Honesty (5.08.04): reporting the full truth, including the parts that are hard to hear: the Declaration’s strong ideas, and the people its words left out in 1776.
  • Courage (5.08.05): printing the names that are easy to leave out — the overlooked contributors, not just the famous ones.
  • Courage (5.08.06): reporting both what women did in the Revolution and what they were still denied afterward, and saying plainly when a famous story is only partly true.

“Big words like ‘every’ are easy to write and hard to prove.”Ms. Avery, when your student’s front page uses a word like every, all, or only

Good to know: The lessons are honest about who paid for this history. In 5.08.01, students learn that Native nations already lived in the Ohio Valley that Britain and France fought over, and that no one asked them. Native nations chose sides for their own reasons: many helped France, and the Mohawk often helped Britain. After the war, Native nations led in part by the Odawa leader Pontiac fought British forts (1763), and the king’s Proclamation of 1763 set the land west of the Appalachian Mountains aside for Native nations, which angered colonists who wanted to move there. The lesson tells this calmly and plainly, with no graphic detail. The Lake George print shows a battle from a distance, with no close-up violence. The Hasty Herald’s headline and the lesson’s summary lines were written for the lesson; the 1756 print and the king’s 1763 words are real. In 5.08.02, the Boston Tea Party is named as a protest (342 chests of tea dumped in Boston Harbor) with no violence shown. The sealed “invitation” on the first tab was written for the lesson; the 1766 Boston broadside it is based on is real. In 5.08.03, students learn plainly that some enslaved people joined the British after a 1775 offer of freedom, that many died of disease, and that a Patriot army burned dozens of Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) towns in 1779. The 1775 London cartoon shows a threat of tar and feathers; no one is hurt in the picture. The knock at the door and the two short speeches were written for the lesson, and the page says so. In 5.08.04, students learn plainly that in 1776 the Declaration’s words did not include enslaved people, women or Native peoples, that its list of grievances used insulting words for Native peoples, and that Thomas Jefferson enslaved more than 600 people during his life. They also see people later use the same words to ask for their rights. The eraser game and the plain-language lines were written for the lesson; the Declaration’s words and the 1777 printing are real. In 5.08.05, students learn that free and enslaved Black soldiers served in the Revolution, including the First Rhode Island Regiment, and that Wentworth Cheswell was the grandson of an enslaved man. The lesson sticks to Cheswell’s documented record (messenger rides, the Saratoga campaign, elected town offices) and does not repeat the undocumented story that he rode with Paul Revere the same night. The empty frames and the chain game were written for the lesson; the 1781 drawing is real. In 5.08.06, students sort famous stories about women into what is documented and what is legend. Deborah Sampson’s 17 months of army service, the Daughters of Liberty and the women who traveled with the army are on record. “Molly Pitcher” is part legend (Mary Ludwig Hays is the woman most often linked to it), and “Agent 355” rests on a single line in one 1779 spy letter, so the lesson does not present either as plain fact. Students also learn calmly that after the war most women still could not vote or hold office; New Jersey let some women who owned property vote until 1807. The grandstand and the stamps were written for the lesson; Abigail Adams’s 1776 letter is real, and the Molly Pitcher painting is real but was made about 1911, long after the war.

Lesson by Lesson

Issue 37 · 5.08.01

French and Indian War

Courage
The question

Which event changed what came next, and how do we know?

  1. Hold it to the light. The Herald’s headline jumps from the end of the war (1763) straight to 1776. Your student lights up the 13 empty years in between and seals a guess about what filled them.
  2. Set the missing middle. After a short reading, your student sets lines of type in a printer’s composing stick, in order: Britain wins but owes a huge war debt, the Proclamation line, Parliament’s taxes, and colonists’ protests. Each line needs a reason why it comes next. Then they decode one real sentence from the king’s Proclamation of 1763, phrase by phrase.
  3. Study a real print. An engraving of the Battle of Lake George (1755), published in London in 1756. Its title says 250 Mohawks fought with the English, and French soldiers fought with their own Native allies. Your student works out what one battle picture can show, and what it can’t.
Word root: claim / clam = call out
What your student learns

Britain and France both claimed the Ohio Valley, where Native nations already lived. The fighting began in 1754 at Fort Necessity, and the Treaty of Paris (1763) gave Britain nearly all French land east of the Mississippi. Winning left Britain deep in debt, so Parliament taxed the colonies, and the Proclamation of 1763 blocked settling west of the mountains. Colonists had no representatives in Parliament, and in 1776 they explained their rights in the Declaration of Independence.

What they write

A Front Page Brief: a short front-page story with a clear claim about how the war changed what came next, evidence, and why it matters.

Issue 38 · 5.08.02

Parliamentary Acts and Colonial Grievances

Courage
The question

What does this source say, what can we infer, and what must we still question?

  1. Break the seal. A sealed invitation (written for the lesson) to Boston’s 1766 “Day of General Rejoicing” after Parliament canceled the Stamp Act. Your student opens it in three taps and seals a guess: if the tax was gone, why was Boston still talking about a right?
  2. Purse or ballot box. After a short reading on four acts (Sugar Act, Stamp Act, Townshend Acts, Tea Act), your student sorts short lines from each act into a coin purse (it cost money) or a ballot box (it took away a say or a right). Each act hits both. A card adds the Declaratory Act of 1766.
  3. Read a real broadside. Your student sorts four lines from Boston’s 1766 town-meeting broadside onto ribbons: thanks the King, claims a right, plans the party. Then they mark up the broadside with the red pencil and work out what it can and can’t show.
Word root: grav / griev = heavy
What your student learns

To help pay its war debt, Parliament taxed the colonies: the Sugar Act (1764), the Stamp Act (1765), the Townshend Acts (1767) and the Tea Act (1773). Colonists had no vote in Parliament, so they argued that Parliament had no right to tax them: “No Taxation Without Representation.” They protested with petitions, boycotts and the Stamp Act Congress, and later the Boston Tea Party. When the Stamp Act was repealed in 1766, Boston celebrated, thanked the King, and still claimed its right to tax itself.

What they write

A Gazette Correction: a short piece that quotes the Herald’s claim, gives evidence from the acts or the broadside, and rewrites the claim so it is accurate and fair.

Issue 39 · 5.08.03

Patriots, Loyalists, and Other Colonists

Courage
The question

Was every colonist really either a Patriot or a Loyalist, or was choosing a side more complicated?

  1. A knock at the door. It is 1774, and a town committee (written for the lesson) asks your student to sign a pledge to buy no British goods. They pick Sign, Refuse or Let me think, and then see what every choice could cost.
  2. A street of doors. After a short reading on Patriots, Loyalists and neutral colonists, your student opens five doors: a Boston printer, a Virginia merchant, a Quaker farm family, an enslaved man in Virginia, and a Haudenosaunee family. Each has one reason and one cost, and your student hangs a sign on each door (Patriot, Loyalist, Neutral or It’s complicated). A card tells how the Revolution split Benjamin Franklin and his son William.
  3. Who made this picture? Your student reads who made a real 1775 London cartoon, turns a dial to say whose side its makers were on, and then marks it up with the red pencil: what it shows, and what it can’t show.
Word root: patr = father
What your student learns

Colonists were divided. Historians estimate that about 40–45% were Patriots, 15–20% were Loyalists, and about 35–40% tried to stay neutral (no one counted at the time). Each group had real reasons: rights and self-government, loyalty and order, faith and safety. Enslaved people and Native nations made their own hard choices, often about freedom and land. Every choice had a cost.

What they write

A Front Page Brief: a short front-page story with a clear claim about how colonists were divided, evidence that names a group and its reason, and why it matters.

Issue 40 · 5.08.04

Declaration of Independence: Unalienable Rights

Honesty
The question

Was the Declaration of Independence just a list of complaints, or an argument about rights?

  1. Try to erase it. Your student tries to rub out “Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness” on a scrap of paper. The words won’t come off, which is what unalienable means: rights no one can take away.
  2. Build the arch. After a short reading, your student taps five stones, each with a real line from the Declaration, and decides each one’s job: the big idea, evidence, “we tried peace,” or the decision. Then they pull out the keystone and watch the arch fall: without the big idea, the complaints are just complaints. A card tells who wrote it, and a calm last stop shows who the words left out in 1776 and who used them later.
  3. A real printing from 1777. Your student looks closely at the top and bottom of the copy printed by Mary Katharine Goddard, then marks it up with the red pencil: what it shows, and what it can’t show.
  4. Copywork. Your student copies one line of the Declaration by hand in their notebook.
Word root: clar = clear
What your student learns

The Declaration is an argument with parts. It says people are born with unalienable rights, that governments get their power from the consent of the governed, and that the long list of grievances (such as taxes without consent) is the evidence. The colonists say they asked for peace first, and the last part announces independence. In 1776, the words did not reach everyone, and people who were left out later used the same words to ask for their rights.

What they write

A Gazette Correction: a short piece that quotes the Herald’s claim, gives evidence from the Declaration’s real words, and rewrites the claim so it is accurate and fair.

Issue 41 · 5.08.05

Significant Individuals and Groups in the Revolution

Courage
The question

Was the Revolution led by just a few famous men, or did many different people and groups make independence possible?

  1. Tap the empty frames. The Herald’s front page has one portrait of Washington and five frames marked “nobody important.” Your student taps each frame and a real contributor appears: Wentworth Cheswell, Haym Salomon, the Sons of Liberty, ordinary soldiers and Benjamin Franklin.
  2. Link the contributors. After six short profile cards, your student makes each contributor a link in a chain holding the word INDEPENDENCE, choosing what each gave: fighting, money, messages, organizing, or help from allies. Then they pull one link out and watch the chain sag — take away one kind of help, and independence hangs by a thread. A calm card notes that Patriots, Loyalists and neutral colonists disagreed, and a short stop compares how people took part then and now.
  3. A real drawing from 1781. Your student marks up a watercolor by a French officer showing four American soldiers, including a First Rhode Island Regiment soldier, then sorts what the drawing can show, can’t show, and doesn’t show.
Word root: cor = heart
What your student learns

Independence was won by a coalition: Washington and thousands of ordinary soldiers fought; Haym Salomon raised the money; Wentworth Cheswell carried war news and was elected to town office; the Sons of Liberty organized; Franklin brought France in as an ally. Free and enslaved Black soldiers served, including the First Rhode Island Regiment. Not every colonist agreed, and choosing a side took courage.

What they write

A Front Page Brief: a clear claim about who contributed, evidence naming at least two people or groups and what each gave, and why the full story matters.

Issue 42 · 5.08.06

Changing Roles and Impact of Women During the Revolution

Courage
The question

Did women really just watch the Revolution happen, or does the evidence show they played roles the history books often leave out?

  1. Empty the grandstand. The Herald drew women sitting in a grandstand, “watching the war.” Your student taps each seat and the woman leaves for her real work: running farms and shops, spinning cloth for the boycott, cooking and nursing for the army, serving as a soldier, writing to the leaders.
  2. Record or legend? Six short story cards, each with its proof. Your student stamps each one Documented, Part legend or Not sure. Then they read Abigail Adams’s real 1776 letter (“Remember the Ladies”) and a calm card on what women still could not do after the war.
  3. A painting made in 1911. Your student marks up Percy Moran’s famous Molly Pitcher painting, finds that it was made 133 years after the battle, and sorts what a painting made that late can and can’t prove.
Word root: spect = look
What your student learns

Women ran farms, shops and trades; the Daughters of Liberty boycotted British goods; thousands of camp followers cooked, washed and nursed for the army; Deborah Sampson served as a soldier. Some famous stories are part legend, and a careful editor says so. After the war, most women still could not vote or hold office.

What they write

A Gazette Correction: a short piece that quotes the Herald’s claim, names at least two roles women played with proof for each, and rewrites the claim so it is accurate and fair.

Ask at Home

Better than “How was school?”
  1. Did the Revolution start right after the French and Indian War?Listen for: no. The war ended in 1763 and the Declaration came in 1776. In between came Britain’s war debt, the Proclamation line, new taxes, and colonists’ protests.
  2. Britain won the war. Why weren’t colonists happy?Listen for: Britain taxed them to help pay the debt, they had no vote in Parliament, and the Proclamation of 1763 said they couldn’t settle west of the mountains.
  3. Did all Native nations fight on the same side?Listen for: no. They chose sides for their own reasons. Many helped France, and the Mohawk often helped Britain.
  4. Were the colonists just mad about paying money?Listen for: partly, but they also said Parliament had no right to tax them because they had no vote there. “No Taxation Without Representation.”
  5. When the Stamp Act was canceled, was the argument over?Listen for: no. Boston thanked the King but still claimed its right, Parliament passed the Declaratory Act, and new taxes came in 1767.
  6. Did every colonist pick Patriot or Loyalist?Listen for: no. Many tried to stay neutral, like Quakers who would not fight, and both sides pushed them.
  7. Why might an enslaved man in 1775 join the British?Listen for: Virginia’s British governor promised freedom. His choice was about his own freedom, not the king or taxes.
  8. Was the Declaration just a list of complaints?Listen for: no. The complaints (grievances) were evidence for a big idea: people have rights no one can take away, and governments get their power from the people.
  9. Did “all men are created equal” include everyone in 1776?Listen for: no. It left out enslaved people, women and Native peoples. Later, people used those same words to ask for their rights.
  10. Who helped win the Revolution besides the famous leaders?Listen for: soldiers and militias, money-raisers like Haym Salomon, riders like Wentworth Cheswell, organizers like the Sons of Liberty, and allies like France.
  11. What is the difference between a documented story and a legend? Can you give one of each from the Revolution?Listen for: documented means written down at the time, like Deborah Sampson’s army records; a legend grew as people retold it, like Molly Pitcher.

How Learning Is Checked

Each lesson’s front page, notebook and Fact-Check show how your student is thinking. At the end of the unit there will be a short Canvas quiz, Unit 8 Check: Road to Revolution, about the unit’s lessons, mostly multiple choice. Some questions ask your student to reason like an editor: which detail supports a claim, and what a source can or can’t show. There is one try, and your student sees the right answers and a short reason after submitting.

📬

What Gets Turned In

  • Editor’s Notebook: 3 short written answers on the Desk tab
  • Fact-Check: 3 questions, plus 1 or 2 harder claims with a call and a reason
  • A front page (in 5.08.01, 5.08.03 and 5.08.05, a Front Page Brief; in 5.08.02, 5.08.04 and 5.08.06, a Gazette Correction): a headline, three short parts in your student’s own words, and a corrected version of the Herald’s claim
  • At least 2 pieces of evidence, each with a sentence saying what it proves
  • An Archive Line: one careful sentence for future citizens to remember
  • Copied, downloaded, or saved as a PDF, then submitted in Canvas

How to help without doing it for them: ask what the Herald got wrong and which detail proves it, then let your student do the writing. Spelling can wait; the reasons can’t.

Done well looks like: real details, not just names, and evidence that’s explained. As Ms. Avery says, “Picking cards isn’t the work. Explaining them is.”

Try at Home

5 minutes
  • Find the middle. Pick a family story with a beginning and an end. What happened in between? Which step led to the next?
  • Who paid? Talk about a time something was won or bought. Who paid for it, and how did they feel?
  • Made vs. shown. Find an old photo or a picture in a book. When was it made? What does it show? What can’t it show?
  • Purse or say? Think of a family rule. Does it cost money, or does it change who gets to decide? Which one would you argue about more?
  • Every choice has a cost. Pick a small choice your family made this week. What was one reason for it, and one thing it cost?
  • Words that last. Read “Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness” together. Which word matters most to each of you, and why?
  • Our own chain. Think of something your family pulled off together. Who gave what — time, ideas, rides, snacks? What would have happened if one link was missing?
  • Record or legend at home. Pick a family story everyone tells. Is there a photo, a letter or a date that proves it, or has it grown a little with each retelling?
