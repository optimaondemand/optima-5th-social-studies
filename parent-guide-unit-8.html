At a Glance

Unit 8 follows the road from the French and Indian War to the Declaration of Independence. It starts with a war the colonists fought on Britain’s side and asks what changed afterward. Your student learns to look for the chain of events between big moments, instead of jumping straight from one date to the next.

History skill
Find the chain, not just the dates. What happened, what happened because of it, and how do we know? Your student also checks what a source can and can’t show (the lessons call this a limitation), and notices when a picture was made compared with the day it shows.
Virtues
  • Courage (5.08.01): printing what the evidence shows, even when the true story is harder to tell than the simple one.

“Big words like ‘every’ are easy to write and hard to prove.”Ms. Avery, when your student’s front page uses a word like every, all, or only

Good to know: The lessons are honest about who paid for this history. In 5.08.01, students learn that Native nations already lived in the Ohio Valley that Britain and France fought over, and that no one asked them. Native nations chose sides for their own reasons: many helped France, and the Mohawk often helped Britain. After the war, Native nations led in part by the Odawa leader Pontiac fought British forts (1763), and the king’s Proclamation of 1763 set the land west of the Appalachian Mountains aside for Native nations, which angered colonists who wanted to move there. The lesson tells this calmly and plainly, with no graphic detail. The Lake George print shows a battle from a distance, with no close-up violence. The Hasty Herald’s headline and the lesson’s summary lines were written for the lesson; the 1756 print and the king’s 1763 words are real.

Lesson by Lesson

Issue 37 · 5.08.01

French and Indian War

Courage
The question

Which event changed what came next, and how do we know?

  1. Hold it to the light. The Herald’s headline jumps from the end of the war (1763) straight to 1776. Your student lights up the 13 empty years in between and seals a guess about what filled them.
  2. Set the missing middle. After a short reading, your student sets lines of type in a printer’s composing stick, in order: Britain wins but owes a huge war debt, the Proclamation line, Parliament’s taxes, and colonists’ protests. Each line needs a reason why it comes next. Then they decode one real sentence from the king’s Proclamation of 1763, phrase by phrase.
  3. Study a real print. An engraving of the Battle of Lake George (1755), published in London in 1756. Its title says 250 Mohawks fought with the English, and French soldiers fought with their own Native allies. Your student works out what one battle picture can show, and what it can’t.
Word root: claim / clam = call out
What your student learns

Britain and France both claimed the Ohio Valley, where Native nations already lived. The fighting began in 1754 at Fort Necessity, and the Treaty of Paris (1763) gave Britain nearly all French land east of the Mississippi. Winning left Britain deep in debt, so Parliament taxed the colonies, and the Proclamation of 1763 blocked settling west of the mountains. Colonists had no representatives in Parliament, and in 1776 they explained their rights in the Declaration of Independence.

What they write

A Front Page Brief: a short front-page story with a clear claim about how the war changed what came next, evidence, and why it matters.

Ask at Home

Better than “How was school?”
  1. Did the Revolution start right after the French and Indian War?Listen for: no. The war ended in 1763 and the Declaration came in 1776. In between came Britain’s war debt, the Proclamation line, new taxes, and colonists’ protests.
  2. Britain won the war. Why weren’t colonists happy?Listen for: Britain taxed them to help pay the debt, they had no vote in Parliament, and the Proclamation of 1763 said they couldn’t settle west of the mountains.
  3. Did all Native nations fight on the same side?Listen for: no. They chose sides for their own reasons. Many helped France, and the Mohawk often helped Britain.

How Learning Is Checked

Each lesson’s front page, notebook and Fact-Check show how your student is thinking. At the end of the unit there will be a short Canvas quiz, Unit 8 Check: Road to Revolution, about the unit’s lessons, mostly multiple choice. Some questions ask your student to reason like an editor: which detail supports a claim, and what a source can or can’t show. There is one try, and your student sees the right answers and a short reason after submitting.

📬

What Gets Turned In

  • Editor’s Notebook: 3 short written answers on the Desk tab
  • Fact-Check: 3 questions, plus 2 harder claims with a call and a reason
  • A front page (in 5.08.01, a Front Page Brief): a headline, three short parts in your student’s own words, and a corrected version of the Herald’s claim
  • At least 2 pieces of evidence, each with a sentence saying what it proves
  • An Archive Line: one careful sentence for future citizens to remember
  • Copied, downloaded, or saved as a PDF, then submitted in Canvas

How to help without doing it for them: ask what the Herald got wrong and which detail proves it, then let your student do the writing. Spelling can wait; the reasons can’t.

Done well looks like: real details, not just names, and evidence that’s explained. As Ms. Avery says, “Picking cards isn’t the work. Explaining them is.”

Try at Home

5 minutes
  • Find the middle. Pick a family story with a beginning and an end. What happened in between? Which step led to the next?
  • Who paid? Talk about a time something was won or bought. Who paid for it, and how did they feel?
  • Made vs. shown. Find an old photo or a picture in a book. When was it made? What does it show? What can’t it show?
