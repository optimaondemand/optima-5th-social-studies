📚 Parliament Passes the Bills, Colonists Push Back

After the French and Indian War, Britain owed a huge amount of money. Parliament (Britain’s lawmakers) decided the colonies should help pay. Starting in 1764, it passed new laws, called acts, that taxed goods and limited colonial trade. Colonists said something bigger was being taken: their rights as British subjects.

Here are the four acts you need to know for today:

1764
Sugar Act
Taxed sugar, molasses and other goods brought in from overseas. People accused of smuggling (sneaking goods in to skip the tax) were tried by a British judge, with no colonial jury.
Why it mattered Colonists said a trial with no local jury broke their rights as British subjects. Some Boston merchants began early boycotts (refusing to buy British goods).
1765
Stamp Act
Required a tax stamp on many printed papers: newspapers, legal papers, even playing cards and dice.
Why it mattered It was the first direct tax Parliament put on the colonists (a tax paid straight to the government, not hidden in a price). It sparked the slogan “No Taxation Without Representation.” Nine colonies sent delegates (people chosen to speak for them) to the Stamp Act Congress, one of the first times colonies worked together.
1767
Townshend Acts
Taxed glass, paper, paint and tea brought from Britain. They also shut down the New York Assembly (the colony’s elected lawmakers) for refusing to house British soldiers.
Why it mattered Women in groups called the Daughters of Liberty held spinning bees to make cloth instead of buying British cloth. Later, in 1772–1773, colonists set up Committees of Correspondence to share news between towns and colonies.
1773
Tea Act
Gave Britain’s East India Company a monopoly (the only right to sell) on tea in the colonies, so it could sell tea cheaper than colonial merchants could. The tea tax stayed in place.
Why it mattered Colonists in Boston dumped 342 chests of British tea into Boston Harbor: the Boston Tea Party.
🗳️ Taking Part Without a Vote

Colonists could not vote for Parliament, so they found other ways to be heard. They signed petitions (written requests to leaders), boycotted British goods, and sent delegates to the Stamp Act Congress in 1765. Today, citizens can vote, contact their representatives, and sign petitions. Many of the colonists’ grievances were later listed in the Declaration of Independence, the founding document that says people have unalienable rights (rights no government can take away).

★ Key Concept: “No Taxation Without Representation”

Colonists weren’t just saying “we don’t want to pay taxes.” They were saying: we have no elected representatives in Parliament, so Parliament has no right to tax us. Being taxed without their consent (agreement) broke their rights as British subjects.

🦦
Opti's Fun Fact: The Stamp Act even taxed playing cards and dice. Some colonists burned stamped paper in bonfires, and crowds hung stamp tax officials in effigy (as stuffed dolls) from Liberty Trees.

The Hasty Herald says colonists protested “only because they disliked paying taxes.” Notice what that leaves out: the argument about consent, representation and rights. A courageous editor looks past the simplest explanation and asks what the source really tells us.

🔎 Read the Source: A Boston Broadside, 1766

A broadside was a large printed sheet, like a poster or flier. Colonists posted broadsides in squares, taverns and shops to spread news. This real one was printed in Boston on May 16, 1766. It shares what a town meeting voted on April 21, 1766, while Boston waited for official news that Parliament had repealed (canceled) the Stamp Act. The news came on May 16. Look at the words the town chose. What did they celebrate, and what did they still insist on?

🔤 The Word Desk: grav / griev means “heavy”

Many English words come from the Latin word gravis, which means heavy. You can spot it as grav or griev.

  • grievance (griev + ance): a heavy wrong that people complain about
  • grievous (griev + ous): heavy and hurtful
  • gravity (grav + ity): the force that makes things heavy and pulls them down

Discovery: In 1766, Boston’s town meeting called the Stamp Act “grievous” to the King’s subjects in America. A grievance is a wrong that feels heavy.

★ Fact-Check Room ★
Before the Gazette Goes to Print
Check each claim carefully. An honest paper publishes only what holds up.
1 Which best explains why colonists said "No Taxation Without Representation"?
A Colonists refused to pay any taxes at all, no matter what.
B Colonists only objected to the Tea Act, not the earlier acts.
C Colonists argued that Parliament had no right to tax them because they had no elected representatives in Parliament.
2 The Stamp Act of 1765 was different from earlier taxes because it was —
A The first direct tax Parliament placed on everyday colonists, hitting newspapers, legal papers, and even playing cards.
B A tax on imported goods that only affected merchants and traders.
C A tax that Parliament repealed the very same year it was passed.
3 An honest editor reviewing the Herald's claim — "Colonists protested only because they disliked paying taxes" — would say the claim is —
A Completely accurate — colonists were only upset about the amount of money they owed.
B Partly true but missing the bigger point — colonists also argued that being taxed without representation violated their rights as British citizens.
C Completely false — colonists never objected to paying taxes of any kind.
