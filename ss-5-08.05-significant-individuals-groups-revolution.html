🗞️ The People Behind the Revolution

The Hasty Herald says a handful of famous men led the Revolution and no one else mattered. The record says something bigger: independence was won by a coalition — a team of different people working for one goal. Soldiers, riders, money-raisers, organizers and diplomats all pulled together, and history has overlooked many of them.

🗞️
Beyond the Famous Few

As you read each card, ask: what did they give, and why does the Herald leave them out?

George Washington

Commander-in-Chief — the top general — of the Continental Army. He led an army short on money, training and supplies against the most powerful military in the world, from the hard winter at Valley Forge to the victory at Yorktown. He could not have won alone.

Benjamin Franklin

A diplomat: a person who speaks for their country to other countries. Franklin talked France into joining the war as an ally — a country on your side. France sent soldiers, ships and money. Without that help, the Revolution might have failed.

Wentworth Cheswell

A teacher and town leader from New Hampshire, and the grandson of an enslaved man. He carried urgent war news on horseback for his town’s safety committee, fought in the Saratoga campaign, and was elected to town office year after year — one of the first men of African descent elected to public office in America.

Haym Salomon

A Jewish immigrant from Poland and a financier: a person who raises money. He raised the money that paid soldiers and bought supplies. The war could not have gone on without money raised by people like Salomon.

The Sons of Liberty

A group of colonists — including Samuel Adams, John Hancock and Paul Revere — who organized protests against British laws. They planned the Boston Tea Party and spread the news in pamphlets, small printed booklets.

Ordinary Soldiers & Militias

Thousands of farmers, tradespeople and young people fought, often without pay or enough food. Militias — ordinary people trained to fight in emergencies — turned out when the war came. Free and enslaved Black soldiers served too, including the First Rhode Island Regiment.

⚖️
Cause — Choice — Consequence

Cause: Winning independence took more than ideas. It took soldiers, money, messages, organizing and allies.

Choice: People from many backgrounds chose to give what they had — their pens, their purses, their horses, their lives.

Consequence: The Revolution was won by a wide coalition, not just a few famous leaders.

⚖️ Not Everyone Agreed

Not every colonist supported the Revolution. Patriots wanted independence, Loyalists wanted to stay with Britain, and many colonists stayed neutral. Choosing a side took courage, because neighbors disagreed. The Declaration of Independence, which promised rights no one can take away, became a symbol of the new country.

🗳️ Taking Part, Then and Now

Fighting wasn’t the only way to take part. In the 1770s, mostly white men who owned land could vote; others joined militias, signed petitions, organized boycotts and carried news. Today, citizens 18 and older can vote, and people still serve, volunteer, write to leaders and report the news. Wentworth Cheswell was elected to town offices almost every year of his adult life.

🦉🗞️
Paul Revere’s ride is famous mostly because of a poem written long afterward. Wentworth Cheswell rode with war news too, and almost no one knows his name. A courageous editor prints both.

Key Concept

The American Revolution was won by a wide coalition of individuals and groups — soldiers, money-raisers, diplomats, riders, organizers and ordinary people — not just a few famous men.

🔤 The Word Desk: cor means “heart”

Many English words come from the Latin word cor, “heart.” You can spot it as cor or cour.

  • courage (cour + age): doing what’s right even when you’re afraid
  • encourage (en + cour + age): to put heart into someone
  • core (cor + e): the heart or center of something

Discovery: Long ago, people believed bravery lived in the heart. Today’s contributors showed courage in different ways: with muskets, money, messages and ink.

🏛️ Texas Connection — Founders & Leadership Qualities (TEKS 18)

Texas students study the Founding Fathers and other national leaders not just for what they did, but for the leadership qualities they demonstrated — courage, integrity, persuasion, and willingness to compromise. Consider: George Washington stepped down from power voluntarily. Benjamin Franklin, at 81, brokered compromises between bitter rivals at the Constitutional Convention. These weren't just political decisions — they were character decisions that shaped the nation.

A He made a midnight ride to warn colonists of British forces, served as a soldier and town official, yet is often left out of the story.
B He wrote the Declaration of Independence.
C He was the commander of the Continental Army.
3 What would a courageous editor include when reporting on who led the Revolution?
A Only the most famous leaders, because readers already know their names.
B The full range of contributors — famous and overlooked — with evidence of what each group contributed.
