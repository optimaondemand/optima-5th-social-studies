📚 Many Lives, One Colony

Daily life in the Thirteen Colonies looked very different depending on who you were. A wealthy Southern planter (the owner of a plantation, a very large farm) lived in a big house, made enslaved people work his fields, and helped make the colony’s laws. An enslaved person on that same plantation was forced to work from sunrise to sunset with no pay, no freedom, and no legal rights (rights that the law protects). Their work built the planter’s wealth. A New England farmer worked a small, rocky farm, went to town meetings, and often sent children to a one-room schoolhouse. A Philadelphia craftsman, such as a blacksmith, a printer, or a barrel-maker, ran a workshop and trained apprentices (young workers learning a trade).

Women in every colony ran their households. They cooked, preserved food so it would last all winter, made cloth and clothing, kept gardens, and cared for children. Women could not vote, and the law gave most married women few rights of their own. Children worked alongside their parents from a young age. In New England, some children went to school. In the South, school depended on whether a family was rich. Enslaved children were made to work in the fields or in the planter’s house. The colonies were not one experience. They were many experiences, shaped by where people lived, how much money they had, their race, and their gender (whether they were men or women). An honest editor reports all of them.

🏠 Free Colonists
Varied by Region and Wealth
Free colonists' daily lives depended on where they lived and how much they owned — but they had legal rights and choices.
Examples NE farmer: small farm, town meetings, church · Planter: large estate, political power · Craftsman: workshop, apprentices, trade · Women: households, no vote
⛓️ Enslaved People
No Freedom, No Choice
Enslaved people had no legal rights, no pay, and no freedom — their labor built the colonial economy, especially in the South.
Daily reality Forced labor sunrise to sunset · No pay, no legal rights · Families separated · Children worked in fields or houses · Violence enforced obedience

The Hasty Herald said daily life was the same for everyone. But a planter’s day looked nothing like an enslaved person’s day, a craftsman’s day looked nothing like a farmer’s, and a woman’s day was different from a man’s. An editor practicing self-governance (being in charge of your own choices) reports the full range.

🔤 The Word Desk: vers means “turn”

Many English words come from the Latin root vers (or vert), which means turn.

  • diverse (di + vers): turned in different directions, so many different kinds
  • reverse (re + vers): to turn back
  • versus: turned against, like Team A versus Team B
  • anniversary (anni + vers): the day the year turns around again

Discovery: The kitchen picture on this tab was printed in 1876, the 100th anniversary year of the Declaration of Independence. The year had turned around one hundred times since 1776.

Try it: use the word diverse in a sentence about colonial daily life.
