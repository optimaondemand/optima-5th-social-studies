At a Glance

Unit 7 starts Colonial America. In these lessons your student learns that people came to the colonies for many different reasons, and some didn’t choose to come at all. They see how New England’s rocky land and Puritan beliefs shaped the towns there. Then they follow six people through one day and find out how different those days were, depending on where people lived, how much money they had, their race, and whether they were men or women. The unit ends by putting the three regions side by side.

History skill
Check a source before you trust it. Who made it, when, and for whom? Your student learns that a picture can be made long after the time it shows, and names one thing each source can’t show (the lessons call this a limitation).
Virtues
  • Courtesy (5.07.01): treating every person with respect.
  • Courage (5.07.02): telling the whole truth, even when a simple answer would be easier.
  • Service (5.07.03): giving readers the whole story so they can understand a place and its people.
  • Perseverance (5.07.04): keeping on with a story when it is hard, and asking who did the work and who was left out.
  • Self-governance (5.07.05): being in charge of your own choices.
  • Responsibility (5.07.06): naming the people who paid the price, not just the goods and the money.
  • Honesty (5.07.07): telling the whole story, including how enslaved people loved, resisted and built free lives.
  • Courtesy (5.07.08): giving each region its own story instead of squeezing thirteen colonies into one.

“Big words like ‘every’ are easy to write and hard to prove.”Ms. Avery, when your student’s front page uses a word like every, all, or only

Good to know: The lessons are honest about hard parts of this history. Enslaved Africans were brought by force, slavery was legal in all thirteen colonies (New England too), and every colony was built on land where Native nations already lived. In 5.07.04, students learn that in South Carolina in 1750 about 61 of every 100 people were enslaved, that enslaved people did most of the work on big plantations and got nothing for it, and that colonial traders also enslaved Native people. In 5.07.03, students read how William Penn bought land from the Lenape with deeds, and how his sons later took about 1,200 square miles in the 1737 Walking Purchase. In 5.07.06, students learn about the Middle Passage, the forced voyage of enslaved Africans across the Atlantic: about 12.5 million people were forced onto slave ships, and about 1 in 7 died on the way. The lesson describes it calmly and plainly, with no graphic pictures, and students read two short passages from Olaudah Equiano, who lived through it. The six people in 5.07.05 are examples based on real history, not real diaries, and the lesson says so. So is the ship clerk’s account book in 5.07.06. In 5.07.07, students read real colonial laws, such as Virginia’s 1662 law that made the children of enslaved mothers enslaved for life and South Carolina’s 1740 law that banned drums, gatherings and learning to write. They also learn how people answered those laws: by keeping families and culture alive, running away, rising up at Stono in 1739, and building Fort Mose, a free Black town in Spanish Florida, in 1738. The lesson mentions that people died in the Stono Rebellion, without graphic detail. The Hasty Herald’s story with pieces cut out of it was written for the lesson. The one photo in 5.07.07 is real, but it was taken about 1860, long after colonial times, and the lesson teaches students to notice that. The plain-English versions of the two old ads in 5.07.01 were also written for the lesson. In 5.07.08, the chart of the three regions was written for the lesson from Unit 7’s earlier issues; the 1755 map is real.

Lesson by Lesson

Issue 29 · 5.07.01

Motives for Colonial Settlement

Courtesy
The question

Did colonists really settle for the same reasons in every colony?

  1. Read two real ads. Colonies had to find people, so they printed ads. Your student reads two (1609 and 1681) and taps what each one was selling.
  2. Bring in seven ships, one at a time, from St. Augustine, Florida (1565) to Savannah, Georgia (1733). For each ship: who came, why they came, and who already lived there. Your student sorts the reasons into columns: money and profit, faith, trade, guarding the land, a fresh start, and forced to come.
  3. Study the oldest picture of St. Augustine, printed in London in 1589. It was made for an English book, so it shows the town from the attackers’ side.
Word root: mot / mov = move
What your student learns

Spain started St. Augustine in 1565, 42 years before Jamestown. Fewer than half of the Mayflower’s 102 passengers were Separatists. Indentured servants signed a contract to work 4 to 7 years to pay for the trip. About 20 Africans on the White Lion were captured in Angola and forced to come.

What they write

A front page comparing why two colonies began.

Issue 30 · 5.07.02

New England Colonies

Courage
The question

Did New England grow this way just because people preferred it?

  1. Build a town on New England land. The land “talks back”: a big tobacco plantation won’t work on thin, rocky soil with a short summer, but a small farm, a fishing wharf, a sawmill, and a shipyard will.
  2. Build three chains, one link at a time: the land and the work it allowed, Puritan beliefs and town life (reading the Bible, schools, town meetings), and the people who had no say.
  3. Study a photo of a real 1681 meetinghouse and sort what they can see (observation) from what they’re guessing (inference).
What your student learns

Rocky soil pushed people to fishing, lumber, shipbuilding, and trade. Puritans started schools so children could read the Bible: Boston in 1635, Harvard College in 1636. Only men voted at town meetings. Roger Williams was expelled for saying the government should not make rules about religion, and started Providence, Rhode Island in 1636.

What they write

A Gazette Correction that rewrites the Herald’s claim so it is accurate and fair.

Issue 31 · 5.07.03

Middle Colonies

Service
The question

Were the Middle Colonies important only because of where they were?

  1. Look through a spyglass at a real 1750s picture of Philadelphia and log what they spot: ocean ships, long wharves, a windmill that ground grain, and steeples of many different churches.
  2. Weigh the Herald’s claim on a balance scale, one fact at a time: is it about where the region sat, or about what it had (rich soil, grain and flour, people from many homelands)? A box under the scale holds the people who didn’t share in it.
  3. Flip a “then and later” card about William Penn and the Lenape, then study the picture of Philadelphia more closely.
Word root: col / cult = farm
What your student learns

New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania and Delaware were called the breadbasket colonies. They were the most diverse region: Dutch, Swedish, English, German, Scots-Irish, Jewish and African people lived there. Penn’s 1701 charter let people who believed in God worship freely, but only Christians could hold office, and slavery was legal.

What they write

A Front Page Brief that makes a clear claim about why the region mattered, with evidence.

Issue 32 · 5.07.04

Southern Colonies

Perseverance
The question

Were the Southern Colonies really just farms and plantations?

  1. Follow one barrel of tobacco from a Virginia seedbed to a London dock. At each stop your student decides who did the work, and an account book fills in who did the work and who got paid.
  2. Count 100 people who stand for South Carolina in 1750, one group at a time (planters, small farmers, town tradespeople, enslaved field and skilled workers, and the Native nations the count left out), then compare four other colonies.
  3. Study a real picture from 1755, a tobacco wharf drawn in the corner of a map of Virginia, and compare it with an engraving made 100 years later.
Word root: port = carry
What your student learns

Virginia, Maryland, the Carolinas and Georgia grew tobacco, rice and indigo to sell. Enslaved people did most of the work on big plantations; planters, merchants and the King’s tax collectors got the money. Most free families were small farmers, not rich planters. Slavery was legal in every colony, but the South depended on it most. Many enslaved people brought rice-growing skill from West Africa.

What they write

A Gazette Correction that says who did the work and who got the money, with evidence.

Issue 33 · 5.07.05

Daily Life in the Thirteen Colonies

Self-governance
The question

Was daily life in the colonies the same for everyone?

  1. Check the Herald’s picture. With the caption covered, your student guesses what time the kitchen scene shows and whether it might have been drawn later.
  2. Follow six people from sunrise to midday to evening: a wealthy planter in Virginia, an enslaved person on the same plantation, a Massachusetts farm family, a printer’s apprentice in Philadelphia, a farm wife and mother, and a child in New England.
  3. Read a real almanac. Most families had no clock. An almanac listed sunrise and sunset. Your student counts the daylight (about 15 hours in June, about 9 in December) and sorts who got to choose their own hours.
Word root: vers = turn
What your student learns

The kitchen picture was made about 1876 but shows about 1776, so the artist imagined it. Women did hard, skilled work but could not vote. An apprentice signed a contract for a set number of years and then was free. An enslaved person was held as property for life, and their children were born enslaved.

What they write

A front page comparing two people’s days at the same hour.

Issue 34 · 5.07.06

Triangular Trade

Responsibility
The question

Was the triangular trade just ordinary business?

  1. Check a clerk’s account book. A ship’s clerk (in an example written for the lesson) wrote each leg of the voyage in business words, like “300 head of cargo.” Your student picks the honest version of each page, and seals a guess about where most captives were taken.
  2. Follow the routes on a map. For each route: what was carried, who got the money, and who paid the price. A second layer adds New England’s rum and molasses routes and the direct route between Brazil and Africa.
  3. Check two sources. A modern teaching map (made in 2025, so not a source from the past), then two short passages from Olaudah Equiano’s 1789 book, compared with the clerk’s account book.
Word root: merc = trade
What your student learns

Goods from Europe bought captives in Africa; enslaved Africans were forced across the Middle Passage; crops they grew, like sugar and tobacco, went back to Europe. Most captives were taken to the Caribbean and Brazil; about 388,000 to mainland North America. New England was part of the trade too. Business words can hide people.

What they write

A Gazette Correction that rewrites the Herald’s claim so it names who paid the price.

Issue 35 · 5.07.07

Slavery in the Colonies and African American Life

Honesty
The question

Was slavery in the colonies just about work?

  1. Put back what the Herald cut. The Herald’s story (written for the lesson) has six pieces cut out: a law, families, the Stono Rebellion, culture, free Black colonists and Fort Mose. Your student pastes each piece back, then seals an answer: why would a paper cut these parts?
  2. The law said. People answered. Five cards. Each starts with a real colonial law, then flips to show how people answered it, from keeping family names to building a free town in Florida.
  3. Check a real photo. A stereograph (two photos that look 3D in a viewer) taken about 1860. Your student looks through a stereoscope on screen, marks the photo up, and works out what a photo from 1860 can and can’t show about colonial times.
Word root: dign = worthy
What your student learns

Slavery took away freedom and dignity, not just hours of work: enslaved people were owned for life, and so were their children. By 1750 it was legal in all thirteen colonies. Enslaved people resisted in big and small ways and kept their families and culture alive. Some Black colonists were free, and Fort Mose (1738) in Spanish Florida was the first legally free Black town in what is now the United States.

What they write

A Front Page Brief: a short front-page story with a claim, evidence from both the laws and the people’s answers, and why it matters.

Issue 36 · 5.07.08

Colonial Region Comparison

Courtesy
The question

Can one region stand for all thirteen colonies?

  1. Zoom out. The Herald runs one photo, the Massachusetts meetinghouse from Issue 30, and calls it “Colonial America.” Your student slides a bar until the photo is a tiny pin on a map of all thirteen colonies, then seals an answer: what does one photo leave out?
  2. Same question, three answers. Your student asks New England, the Middle Colonies and the Southern Colonies the same five questions (land, work, workers, founders, and whether slavery was legal) and decides each time whether the answers are the same or different. The answers point back to the earlier issues.
  3. Check a real map. John Mitchell’s map, made in London in 1755. Your student pins the three regions on it, marks it up, and works out what a map can show (where places were) and what it can’t (how people lived, or who did the work).
Word root: geo = earth
What your student learns

Geography shaped each region: rocky soil and the sea in New England, rich soil and wheat in the Middle Colonies, a warm climate and cash crops in the South. Different people founded and ran them. The regions still shared some things (slavery was legal in all thirteen by 1750) and traded with each other: a visitor in 1748 saw New York ships carrying flour to Boston and South Carolina and bringing rice back.

What they write

A Gazette Correction: a fix the paper prints when a story was wrong. It names the Herald’s claim, compares two regions with evidence, and rewrites the claim so it is fair.

Ask at Home

Better than “How was school?”
  1. Did everyone come to the colonies for the same reason?Listen for: no. Some came for money and profit (Jamestown), some for faith (Plymouth, Pennsylvania), some for trade, some to guard land (St. Augustine), some for a fresh start (Georgia). And some, like the Africans on the White Lion, didn’t choose to come.
  2. Which came first, St. Augustine or Jamestown?Listen for: St. Augustine, Florida, in 1565. That’s 42 years before Jamestown (1607).
  3. Why didn’t New England have big plantations?Listen for: thin, rocky soil and a short summer. People turned to fishing, lumber, building ships, and trade.
  4. Who got to vote at a New England town meeting?Listen for: only men. Women, servants, enslaved people, and Native people could not vote.
  5. How is an apprentice different from an enslaved person?Listen for: an apprentice signed a contract for a set number of years and became free when it ended. An enslaved person was held as property for life, and their children were born enslaved.
  6. The kitchen picture: when was it made, and what time does it show?Listen for: it was made about 1876 but shows about 1776, so the artist imagined the scene.
  7. What did the clerk’s account book leave out?Listen for: it called people “cargo.” It didn’t show that they were chained, crowded and grieving, or that many died.
  8. What did the Herald cut out of its story about slavery?Listen for: the people. Laws that took their freedom, their families, their resistance (like Stono), their culture, free Black colonists, and Fort Mose.
  9. Could one photo of a Massachusetts meetinghouse show all thirteen colonies?Listen for: no. The Middle Colonies and the South had different land, work and people. But the regions did share some things, like slavery being legal everywhere, and they traded with each other.

How Learning Is Checked

Each lesson’s front page, notebook and Fact-Check show how your student is thinking. At the end of the unit there is a short Canvas quiz, Unit 7 Check: Colonial America: 10 questions about lessons 5.07.01 to 5.07.08, mostly multiple choice, with one matching question and one “choose all that are right.” Two questions ask your student to reason like an editor: which detail supports a claim, and what a source can or can’t show. There is one try, and your student sees the right answers and a short reason after submitting.

📬

What Gets Turned In

  • Editor’s Notebook: 3 short written answers on the Desk tab
  • Fact-Check: 3 questions, plus 2 harder claims with a call and a reason
  • A front page (in 5.07.02 and 5.07.08, a Gazette Correction): a headline, three short parts in your student’s own words, and a corrected version of the Herald’s claim
  • At least 2 pieces of evidence, each with a sentence saying what it proves
  • An Archive Line: one careful sentence for future citizens to remember
  • Copied, downloaded, or saved as a PDF, then submitted in Canvas

How to help without doing it for them: ask what the Herald got wrong and which detail proves it, then let your student do the writing. Spelling can wait; the reasons can’t.

Done well looks like: real details, not just names, and evidence that’s explained. As Ms. Avery says, “Picking cards isn’t the work. Explaining them is.”

Try at Home

5 minutes
  • Read an ad like an editor. Pick any ad together. Ask Ms. Avery’s two questions: who made this, and what do they want me to do?
  • Count the daylight. Look up today’s sunrise and sunset. How many hours of daylight is that? Compare it to the almanac’s June (about 15) and December (about 9).
  • Made vs. shown. Find an old photo or a picture in a book. When was it made? What does it show? What can’t it show?
