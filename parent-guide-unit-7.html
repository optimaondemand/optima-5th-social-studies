In the Republic Gazette

The story in every lesson

Your student is a Junior Editor at The Republic Gazette, a newspaper that checks before it prints. Each lesson is a new issue of the paper.

Editor-in-ChiefMs. AverySends the day’s story, gives tips, and asks good questions.
The rival paperThe Hasty HeraldPrints fast and checks little. Every lesson, it has already printed a claim that’s too simple.
The archiveThe MorgueThe newspaper’s archive. Every issue your student publishes gets filed there.

Each lesson goes the same way. Your student makes a guess and seals it in an envelope, tests the Herald’s claim against real evidence, opens the envelope to see how the guess held up, then writes a front page and “strikes” the Herald’s claim with a red pencil so the correction prints in its place. The Herald’s claims this unit:

5.07.01 · The Herald printed:“Colonists settled for the same reasons in every colony.”
5.07.02 · The Herald printed:“New England colonies grew the way they did because colonists simply preferred it.”
5.07.05 · The Herald printed:“Daily life in the colonies was the same for everyone.”

At a Glance

Unit 7 starts Colonial America. In these three lessons your student learns that people came to the colonies for many different reasons, and some didn’t choose to come at all. They see how New England’s rocky land and Puritan beliefs shaped the towns there. Then they follow six people through one day and find out how different those days were, depending on where people lived, how much money they had, their race, and whether they were men or women.

History skill
Check a source before you trust it. Who made it, when, and for whom? Your student learns that a picture can be made long after the time it shows, and names one thing each source can’t show (the lessons call this a limitation).
Virtues
  • Courtesy (5.07.01): treating every person with respect.
  • Courage (5.07.02): telling the whole truth, even when a simple answer would be easier.
  • Self-governance (5.07.05): being in charge of your own choices.

“Big words like ‘every’ are easy to write and hard to prove.”Ms. Avery, when your student’s front page uses a word like every, all, or only

Good to know: The lessons are honest about hard parts of this history. Enslaved Africans were brought by force, slavery was legal in all thirteen colonies (New England too), and every colony was built on land where Native nations already lived. The six people in 5.07.05 are examples based on real history, not real diaries, and the lesson says so. The plain-English versions of the two old ads in 5.07.01 were also written for the lesson.

Lesson by Lesson

Issue 29 · 5.07.01

Motives for Colonial Settlement

Courtesy
The question

Did colonists really settle for the same reasons in every colony?

  1. Read two real ads. Colonies had to find people, so they printed ads. Your student reads two (1609 and 1681) and taps what each one was selling.
  2. Bring in seven ships, one at a time, from St. Augustine, Florida (1565) to Savannah, Georgia (1733). For each ship: who came, why they came, and who already lived there. Your student sorts the reasons into columns: money and profit, faith, trade, guarding the land, a fresh start, and forced to come.
  3. Study the oldest picture of St. Augustine, printed in London in 1589. It was made for an English book, so it shows the town from the attackers’ side.
Word root: mot / mov = move
What your student learns

Spain started St. Augustine in 1565, 42 years before Jamestown. Fewer than half of the Mayflower’s 102 passengers were Separatists. Indentured servants signed a contract to work 4 to 7 years to pay for the trip. About 20 Africans on the White Lion were captured in Angola and forced to come.

What they write

A front page comparing why two colonies began.

Issue 30 · 5.07.02

New England Colonies

Courage
The question

Did New England grow this way just because people preferred it?

  1. Build a town on New England land. The land “talks back”: a big tobacco plantation won’t work on thin, rocky soil with a short summer, but a small farm, a fishing wharf, a sawmill, and a shipyard will.
  2. Build three chains, one link at a time: the land and the work it allowed, Puritan beliefs and town life (reading the Bible, schools, town meetings), and the people who had no say.
  3. Study a photo of a real 1681 meetinghouse and sort what they can see (observation) from what they’re guessing (inference).
What your student learns

Rocky soil pushed people to fishing, lumber, shipbuilding, and trade. Puritans started schools so children could read the Bible: Boston in 1635, Harvard College in 1636. Only men voted at town meetings. Roger Williams was expelled for saying the government should not make rules about religion, and started Providence, Rhode Island in 1636.

What they write

A Gazette Correction that rewrites the Herald’s claim so it is accurate and fair.

Issue 33 · 5.07.05

Daily Life in the Thirteen Colonies

Self-governance
The question

Was daily life in the colonies the same for everyone?

  1. Check the Herald’s picture. With the caption covered, your student guesses what time the kitchen scene shows and whether it might have been drawn later.
  2. Follow six people from sunrise to midday to evening: a wealthy planter in Virginia, an enslaved person on the same plantation, a Massachusetts farm family, a printer’s apprentice in Philadelphia, a farm wife and mother, and a child in New England.
  3. Read a real almanac. Most families had no clock. An almanac listed sunrise and sunset. Your student counts the daylight (about 15 hours in June, about 9 in December) and sorts who got to choose their own hours.
Word root: vers = turn
What your student learns

The kitchen picture was made about 1876 but shows about 1776, so the artist imagined it. Women did hard, skilled work but could not vote. An apprentice signed a contract for a set number of years and then was free. An enslaved person was held as property for life, and their children were born enslaved.

What they write

A front page comparing two people’s days at the same hour.

Real Sources on the Desk

Look for these
1609 · 5.07.01Nova BritanniaA recruiting pamphlet for the Virginia Company, printed in London. Old spelling, from the title page: Nova Britannia. Offring most excellent fruites by planting in Virginia. Exciting all such as be well affected to further the same. “Fruites” here means rewards or profits. Robert Johnson. New York Public Library.
1681 · 5.07.01William Penn’s ad for PennsylvaniaPrinted for people who might move there, or send servants there. From the title page: Made publick for the Information of such as are or may be disposed to Transport themselves or Servants into those Parts. William Penn, Some Account of the Province of Pennsilvania in America, London.
Printed 1589 · 5.07.01St. Augustine, by Baptista BoazioThe oldest known printed picture of any town in what is now the United States. It shows the English attack of 1586: the fort, the town, and Drake’s ships. Library of Congress.
Built 1681 · 5.07.02The Old Ship MeetinghouseHingham, Massachusetts. Puritans built it, and people still worship in it today. One picture, three dates: built in 1681, box pews added in 1755, photo taken in the 1900s. Historic American Buildings Survey, Library of Congress.
About 1876 · 5.07.05A kitchen pictureIts caption reads A New England Kitchen. A Hundred Years Ago. It shows chores at home, but it can’t show who in the room was free or enslaved. Print by H. W. Pierce. Library of Congress.
1748 · 5.07.05Poor Richard improvedBenjamin Franklin’s almanac listed “The Rising and Setting of the Sun.” A saying from Poor Richard’s Almanack (1735): Early to bed and early to rise, makes a man healthy wealthy and wise. Printed by B. Franklin in Philadelphia. Library of Congress.

Also in the lessons: the Boston News-Letter (1704), the first newspaper printed regularly in the colonies, and the South Carolina Gazette (1739), the paper Elizabeth Timothy took over. She was the first woman in America to edit and publish a newspaper.

Ask at Home

Better than “How was school?”
  1. Did everyone come to the colonies for the same reason?Listen for: no. Some came for money and profit (Jamestown), some for faith (Plymouth, Pennsylvania), some for trade, some to guard land (St. Augustine), some for a fresh start (Georgia). And some, like the Africans on the White Lion, didn’t choose to come.
  2. Which came first, St. Augustine or Jamestown?Listen for: St. Augustine, Florida, in 1565. That’s 42 years before Jamestown (1607).
  3. Why didn’t New England have big plantations?Listen for: thin, rocky soil and a short summer. People turned to fishing, lumber, building ships, and trade.
  4. Who got to vote at a New England town meeting?Listen for: only men. Women, servants, enslaved people, and Native people could not vote.
  5. How is an apprentice different from an enslaved person?Listen for: an apprentice signed a contract for a set number of years and became free when it ended. An enslaved person was held as property for life, and their children were born enslaved.
  6. The kitchen picture: when was it made, and what time does it show?Listen for: it was made about 1876 but shows about 1776, so the artist imagined the scene.

What Gets Turned In

  • Editor’s Notebook: 3 short written answers on the Desk tab
  • Fact-Check: 3 questions, plus 2 harder claims with a call and a reason
  • A front page (in 5.07.02, a Gazette Correction): a headline, three short parts in your student’s own words, and a corrected version of the Herald’s claim
  • At least 2 pieces of evidence, each with a sentence saying what it proves
  • An Archive Line: one careful sentence for future citizens to remember
  • Copied, downloaded, or saved as a PDF, then submitted in Canvas

Done well looks like: real details, not just names, and evidence that’s explained. As Ms. Avery says, “Picking cards isn’t the work. Explaining them is.”

Try at Home

5 minutes
  • Read an ad like an editor. Pick any ad together. Ask Ms. Avery’s two questions: who made this, and what do they want me to do?
  • Count the daylight. Look up today’s sunrise and sunset. How many hours of daylight is that? Compare it to the almanac’s June (about 15) and December (about 9).
  • Made vs. shown. Find an old photo or a picture in a book. When was it made? What does it show? What can’t it show?
