📚 Many Reasons, Many Colonies

The colonies did not all begin for one reason. A motive is a reason that moves someone to act, and the colonists had many.

St. Augustine, Florida (1565) was a Spanish fort built to guard Spain’s treasure ships and its claim to Florida. Priests came to spread the Catholic faith. Jamestown, Virginia (1607) was started by the Virginia Company, a joint-stock company (a company where many people paid in and hoped to split the profits). Its investors wanted gold and profit. Plymouth, Massachusetts (1620) was settled by Pilgrims. The Separatists among them wanted to leave the Church of England and worship in their own way. Massachusetts Bay (1630) was started by Puritans who wanted to build a model religious community.

The Dutch started New Amsterdam (later New York) as a fur trading center. Pennsylvania (1681) was William Penn’s colony for Quakers, where people were free to worship in different ways. Georgia (1733) was meant to guard South Carolina from Spanish Florida, and to give poor English families a fresh start. Its founders talked about helping debtors (people who owed money), but few debtors actually came.

Not everyone chose to come. Many poor English people came as indentured servants: they signed a contract to work for 4 to 7 years to pay for the trip. Enslaved Africans were brought against their will. The Atlantic slave trade carried millions of people to the Americas, and about 400,000 of them to the land that became the United States. And every colony was built on land where Native nations already lived. Different motives built different colonies, with different work, governments and daily lives.

🚢 Voluntary Settlement
Came by Choice
Colonists who chose to come: for wealth, faith, trade, or a fresh start. Their choices still changed the lives of the Native nations already living there.
Examples Jamestown → profit · Plymouth → faith · New Amsterdam → fur trade · Pennsylvania → freedom of worship · Georgia → a fresh start, and guarding the border
⛓️ Forced Migration
Brought Against Their Will
Enslaved Africans were forced to come and forced to work. Their work shaped the colonies’ farms, towns and daily life.
Key facts Millions brought to the Americas; about 400,000 to the land that became the U.S. · Forced work on plantations, on farms and in towns · No choice in coming · Shaped the economy of every region

The Hasty Herald said one reason fit every colony. But the evidence shows money, faith, trade, guarding land, and force all played a part, and they built very different communities. A courteous editor reports the full range.

💰 The Colonies and the Market Economy

In a market economy, people and businesses, not the government, decide what to make, buy, and sell. Buyers and sellers set prices together, people can own property, and they work to earn a profit (money left over after costs).

The colonies showed these traits. Investors in the Virginia Company risked their money hoping for a profit. Farmers chose crops, like tobacco, that would sell for a good price. Merchants in port towns like Boston and Philadelphia bought and sold goods in busy markets. Britain still made laws about some trade, so it was not a completely free market.

🔤 The Word Desk: mot and mov mean “move”

Many English words come from the Latin root mot (or mov), which means move.

  • motive (mot + ive): a reason that moves a person to act
  • remove (re + mov + e): to move something away
  • motion (mot + ion): moving
  • emotion (e + mot + ion): a feeling that moves you

Discovery: Every ship in this lesson moved people across an ocean. Most were moved by their own motives. Enslaved Africans were moved by force: the motive belonged to someone else.

Try it: use the word motive in a sentence about why people came to the colonies.
